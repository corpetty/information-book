Complexity is the cheapest description. The laws depend on what a description may be.

This is a recap of a Lean 4 library that set out to be a unified theory of complexity, was attacked, and came back narrower and true. Everything on this page marked as proved has been checked by the machine.

K(x) = the least cost of any description that realizes x

Choose what counts as a description, what it costs, and what "realizes" means, and you get Kolmogorov complexity, circuit size, addition chains, source coding, and more.

On its own, that definition excludes nothing: every function is K for some choice. The content appears only as the choice is restricted. Step through the restrictions below and watch which laws switch on.

    How we got here

    Thirteen rounds. The second one did the most good.

    1. A unified theory

      One definition, eight laws, a dozen instances from different fields, all in Lean. It claimed the laws hold "for every setting of the dials".

    2. Adversarial review

      By the author and four independent reviewers. Eight claims were false, five were true but empty, and most of the mathematics was already in the literature. The definition turned out to be a language, not a theory.

    3. Restrict until something is excluded

      Self-delimiting descriptions give a representation theorem (Kraft's inequality and its converse) and make the additive laws true. Domination is given a mechanism: one translator, constant overhead.

    4. A universal prefix machine and Levin's coding theorem

      Restricting further to computable systems produces a universal member. An online Kraft–Chaitin allocator turns "complex implies improbable" from a refuted slogan into a theorem about that machine.

    5. The reuse gap

      How much does sharing parts buy? At most an exponential; nothing when no atom repeats; at most the multiplicity of the atoms in between.

    6. An upper bound, and a conjecture that died

      Binary exponentiation attains the multiplicity bound up to a logarithm. The guess that cost is of order atoms + log multiplicity was refuted by counting, and the refutation is machine-checked.

    7. The additive laws, on one machine

      Conditional complexity K(y | x) is defined. Subadditivity, the chain rule's upper half, the two-part code and the logarithmic growth law all hold for the universal prefix machine, each up to a constant.

    8. Symmetry of information

      K(x, y) = K(x) + K(y | x, K(x)) up to a constant, in both directions. With it, typical microstates really do cost macrostate plus log W.

    9. Closing gaps, and finding company

      The functions that are prefix-machine complexities are characterized, and the two halves of micro = macro + log W are joined in one theorem. A prior-art search turned up an existing Lean 4 library that already covers much of this ground.

    10. Complexity cannot be guessed

      No bounded list of guesses contains K. So K(K(x) | x) is unbounded, and symmetry of information is false with the bare condition x: the counterexample just outside the law is now a theorem.

    11. Shared squarings, and a statement that proved nothing

      Straus's method shares the squarings among all the atoms, and brings the upper bound for monomials within a constant of the lower one when the atoms number about log m. Checking the two against each other exposed a flaw: the lower-bound theorems had allowed the empty monomial, which cannot be built at all, so they were trivially true. They now require every exponent to be at least one. A statement-by-statement comparison with the other Lean library shows that most of the universal-machine layer here is a re-proof.

    12. The worst case is the counting bound

      One construction, three times: build a shared table of everything small, then pay one step per use. For binary strings, for monomials and for Boolean circuits, the hardest object now costs what counting says it must, up to a constant.

    13. Checking the prior art, and one more overreach

      All three of those results are classical: Berstel and Brlek for strings, Straus and Pippenger for monomials, Shannon and Lupanov for circuits. The literature calls the pattern the Shannon effect. I had listed "the counting bound is always attained" as an open question; it is false, and the counterexample is now a theorem. In the free semilattice it is support that binds. And the circuit bounds turn out to be in CSLib already, in Lean, with better constants.

    Try the objects

    Three small machines from the library, runnable here. Each one is the idea behind a theorem.

    The temperature dial

    An object with eight descriptions of different lengths. Weigh each description by q raised to its length and add them up: that sum is Z(q). One dial takes you from complexity, through probability, to plain counting.

    Z(q), the total weight
    Share carried by the shortest description

    In the library: Thermo.pow_le_Z_of_K_le (simple implies probable) holds for every system. The converse does not: it needed the universal prefix machine and Levin's theorem.

    The Kraft–Chaitin allocator

    Ask for codewords one at a time. A request of length n claims a slice of width 2−n of the unit interval and receives a codeword of length n + 1: the aligned half-slice that fits inside it. No codeword is ever a prefix of another, and nothing has to be planned ahead.

      In the library: KraftChaitin.exists_machine. The same decoder, fed Levin's requests, proves the coding theorem; fed those requests rescaled for each condition, it proves symmetry of information.

      What reuse buys

      Build a product of k different atoms, each repeated m times. Without sharing, every copy is paid for. With sharing, a power is built by squaring and each intermediate result is paid for once.

      Gain from sharing
      Proved ceiling on the gain

      In the library: MonoidGen.slK_pow_le, Monomial.slK_add_one_le, gain_le_multiplicity. Set m to 1 and the gain disappears: when nothing repeats, reuse buys nothing (binK_eq_binTreeK_of_le).

        What we're after

        The original aim was one theory of complexity across fields. The review showed that the shared definition is only a common language. The aim that survived is sharper: for each law, find the weakest restriction on descriptions under which it holds, and prove a counterexample just outside it.

        For the information-theoretic laws that classification is now nearly complete, as the ladder shows: every stage has a representation theorem, and every law has a stage where it holds. For reuse it is partial. These are the gaps.

        One standing caveat. Almost every result here is classical in the field it comes from. What the library adds is that the laws are stated once, proved once, checked by a machine, and that the places where they fail are proved as well. It is also not the first formalization. A Lean 4 library covering much of the same ground on prefix complexity already exists, and CSLib has the circuit bounds of Shannon and Lupanov with better constants. The framing by restrictions, the proved counterexamples, and the worst-case bounds for strings and monomials were not found formalized elsewhere, but the searches were short.
