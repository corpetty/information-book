## 1. Argument

**Thesis.** Everything you know arrived compressed and selected; the gates that select are owned and tuned for attention, not truth; the response is to build translators and institutions that can survive that market rather than beat it.

**Does it earn it?** For a distillation, yes; it is the best-written file of the four and the quotes are all real (I checked each against its chapter: L19 → Ch 1 L51; L21 → Ch 5b L41; L25 → Ch 2 L80, Ch 5c L45, Ch 7 L63; L27 → Ch 10 L67, Ch 8 L71; L29 → Ch 11 L63; L31 → Ch 12 L7). Where it sags:

- **The paragraph and the page overlap at the end.** L15's last three sentences ("The book's answer is not to retreat … Not victory but survival …") are the same content as L31, at half the length. A reader going top to bottom hits the prescription twice in 400 words.
- **L31 is one 190-word paragraph carrying four semicolon-separated prescriptions plus a monastery quote plus a closing aphorism.** It is the only place where the page reads as a list dressed as prose.
- **Term dependency.** L29 "the receiver budget" is used as a proper term; L23 introduced only "that budget." L25 says "decoding key" where L21 said "decoding instructions"; a reader can't tell whether these are the same thing (they are).
- L11 (the one-sentence version) is 62 words with three subordinate clauses; it is really two sentences.

**Single structural change.** Make the paragraph purely diagnostic (end it at "…'are these my people?'") and let the page carry the prescription. Then split L31 into two paragraphs: the wrong answer (retreat) and the right one (four rules, one per sentence).
