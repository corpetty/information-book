## 1. Argument

**Thesis.** What travels across a network is decided by gates with criteria, not by transport; and because criteria can be set, selection (unlike transport) can be aimed, which is why manipulation, political economy, and repair are all questions about selection.

**Does it earn it?** Yes. This is the strongest of the three chapters: the three-operation model (l.15–25), the cost/choice asymmetry backed by the Price equation (l.35–49), the medium as frozen selection (l.51–61), the Hammer re-read (l.63–69), and a real engagement with Mercier and O'Connor & Weatherall (l.87–95). It is also the longest and the most self-aware, and both hurt it:

- Three openings. l.7 (the feed) is excellent. l.9 ("Chapter 5 kept pointing past itself ... So here it is") and l.11 (telephone game again) are throat-clearing before the claim at l.13.
- The chapter admits its own order is wrong: l.25 promises the medium "turning out near the end to be the most important," and l.53 says "I called it the most important of the three and then walked past it."
- l.41–45 is ~700 words of the author arguing with a prior draft ("for months I wrote *directionless*"). The Price paragraph (l.45) earns the claim; Maxwell's demon and Landauer (l.43) are a tangent the argument does not need.
- l.79–85 repeats the complexity split that Ch 5 l.30 already made.
- l.97 and l.121 are production notes in body text.
- The steering-wheel claim is stated at l.7, 13, 39, 41, 47, 49, 107, 114.

**Rewrites (4 worst paragraphs).**

*l.9 + l.11 (original, 227 words):* "Chapter 5 kept pointing past itself ... Transport explains what *shape* an idea takes as it moves. It does not explain what moves at all. Chapter 5 leaned on the telephone game ... and this chapter is about what the criteria are, who gets to set them, and why that last question turns out to be the one the rest of the book runs on."
*Rewrite (71 words):* "Transport says what shape an idea takes in transit. It says nothing about whether the idea moves. The telephone game hides this by forcing every player to pass the message. Real networks don't. Each receiver decides whether to pass a thing on, to whom, and in which version, and that decision is a gate with criteria. Selection is the sum of those gates. This chapter is about the criteria and who sets them."

*l.41 (original, 262 words):* "Transport is a cost; it scrambles. The word I had to fight my way to is *un-steerable*, because for months I wrote *directionless* instead ... You cannot aim a cost. You can aim a choice."
*Rewrite (98 words):* "Transport has a direction (loss only ever accumulates) but it cannot be aimed, because it is blind to content: it scrambles a variant the same way whatever the variant says. You can make transport cheaper or dearer, with a share button or a character limit, but you cannot make it favor one idea over another. Favoring means reading the idea, and a blind process doesn't read. Selection is different in kind. It has a *what-it-rewards*, and a criterion is the kind of thing that can be set, tuned, captured, and contested. You cannot aim a cost. You can aim a choice."

*l.43 (original, 255 words):* "One honest correction, because the tidy form of the claim is false ... which is exactly why capture always reaches for the criterion and never the pipe."
*Rewrite (78 words):* "A choice is a cost too. Maxwell's demon only opens a door, and it still pays: Landauer showed the bits it must forget cost energy to erase. Natural selection pays in differential death. So the real split is blind cost against criterial cost, two ways of spending the same dissipation. That matters later, because the criterion is the cheap, light layer riding on a heavy pipe, which is why anyone who wants to capture a network reaches for the criterion."

*l.93 (original, 345 words):* "I think Mercier is right, and I think he sharpens this chapter rather than breaking it ... The two readings compose; Ch 7 carries the working-out."
*Rewrite (139 words):* "Mercier is right, and he helps. The receiver is not a passive endpoint; the receiver is a gate, and vigilance is one of its criteria. So his correction is not 'there is no selection' but 'the receiver's own gate is more truth-tracking than the cynical story assumes.' That is true, and later chapters lean on it. It still does not save us, for two reasons. Vigilance is one criterion competing with identity, threat, and belonging, and the fitness data shows those routinely winning; a working immune system will still pass the meme that flatters its tribe. And the receiver's criteria are not fixed. They are trained by the medium over thousands of repetitions, and a medium can slowly select for receivers who pass things on without checking. Mercier describes the immune system we evolved, not one that cannot be worn down. [[emotional-memetics|Chapter 7]] takes this further."
