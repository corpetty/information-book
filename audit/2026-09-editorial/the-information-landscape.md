# Audit: `content/the-information-landscape.md` (Chapter 1)

3,011 words. This is the calibration chapter and it mostly earns that role: plain, first-person, short declaratives, almost no self-narration. The findings below are mostly about tightening, evidence, and linking; the voice is the target the other chapters should move toward.

## 1. Argument

**Thesis:** Information reaches you through five lossy re-encodings, and at each hop a gate discards most of what arrives on criteria that have nothing to do with truth; the gates are institutional choices, so they can be redesigned.

**Does it earn it?** Yes, largely. The transport walk (35–93) is the best-written stretch in the three files: each stage gets the same four beats (what it is / what's a choice / what's lost / what's gained), so the reader learns the pattern and can predict the next section. The selection half (107–147) is thinner: 127–135 re-lists the mermaid diagram's labels in prose rather than adding anything to them, and 137 then summarizes the list a third time.

**Sag points.**
- 95–105 ("What the pipeline view alone misses") spends 100 words saying "there is also selection" before 107 says it again with a heading. Merge.
- 149–155 (medium) and 157–169 (manufactured content) are both "here is a thread for later." Manufactured content earns its place because Chapter 2's Case 4 depends on it. The medium section could be one paragraph.
- 179–183 closes the chapter twice: a roadmap paragraph, then a recap paragraph.

**Term the reader doesn't have yet:** "institutional carriers" (71), "bridge zone" (177, 181), "fitness landscape" (103, 137), "attention economy" (89). All are used as if already defined. 71 is the worst because it is presented as *the* explanation of the consensus stage.

**Single most valuable structural change:** Fold 95–105 into the head of 107, and cut 179–181, ending on 183. That removes two throat-clearings and lets the chapter end on the strongest sentence it has ("We never see The Out There directly. We see whatever survives the gates...").

### Four paragraph rewrites

**1. Lines 7–9 (original, 128 words):**
> This is the diagram I keep coming back to. I drew it years ago, on a whim... But it was missing a piece, and the back half of this chapter is about that missing piece. / If you've ever watched a careful finding get flattened into a headline, and then into a meme that means almost the opposite, this chapter is the map of how that happens. It starts gentle... No special background needed; I'll build each piece as we go.

Rewrite (58 words):
> If you've ever watched a careful finding get flattened into a headline, and then into a meme that means almost the opposite, this is the map of how that happens. I drew the diagram years ago to trace the path an idea takes from the world to a person. I still think it's mostly right. It was missing a piece.

**2. Lines 97–103 (original, 175 words):**
> So that's the transport pipeline. Each stage is a re-encoding... But the transport pipeline alone is incomplete. It tells you what *happens to* information as it moves through abstraction layers. It does not tell you what gets to move. / At each stage, vast amounts of content fail to advance at all... / What survives each stage is determined by criteria that have very little to do with truth... The deeper question is "what survives the selection gates the information has to pass through."

Rewrite (82 words):
> That's transport: five re-encodings, each one lossy. But it only tells you what happens to information that moves. It says nothing about what gets to move at all. Most measurements are never taken. Most data is never analyzed. Most papers never become theory, most theories never become news, most news never becomes a meme. What survives each hop is decided by criteria local to that hop, and almost none of them are about truth. That's the second pipeline.

**3. Lines 141–143 (original, 154 words):**
> The two pipelines work in series at each stage. Content faces selection first: does it survive the gate? Then compression: does it fit the next medium? Sometimes the order reverses (content gets reshaped to pass selection, which is what advocacy and PR do). Sometimes they happen simultaneously... / A consequence: if you only model transport, you over-estimate how much information actually moves. The pipeline looks like a continuous (if lossy) flow. With selection added, the pipeline looks like a series of bottlenecks, each one discarding most of what it receives. The funnel is much narrower than the transport view suggests.

Rewrite (78 words):
> At every stage both run. Content has to pass the gate, then fit the next medium. Sometimes it's reshaped in advance to pass the gate; that's what PR and advocacy are. Either way, each stage emits a small fraction of what it received, transformed. Model transport alone and you picture a lossy but continuous flow. Add selection and you picture a series of bottlenecks. The second picture is the right one.

**4. Lines 179–183 (original, 156 words):**
> The rest of Part I goes deeper into what gets lost... Part IV is about what to do about it. / But the core picture is here. Reality on one side, memes on the other, five stages of transformation in between, each stage doing both compression and selection. We never see The Out There directly... The book is partly about how to be more honest about that, and partly about how to build infrastructure that does better than what we have now.

Rewrite (63 words):
> That's the core picture. Reality on one side, memes on the other, five hops in between, each hop compressing and selecting. We never see The Out There directly. We see what survived the gates, in whatever shape the medium it arrived through allows. The rest of this book is about living with that honestly, and about building something that does better.
