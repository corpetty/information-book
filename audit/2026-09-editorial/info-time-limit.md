# Audit: `content/info-time-limit.md` (Chapter 3)

2,871 words. Two drafts stitched end to end. Lines 8–29 are an early, loose draft (typos, "All that to say," "That's good enough") and 31–65 are a later, denser one written in the vocabulary of Chapters 8–12. The seam is visible at 33, and the author admits it at 83. Neither half is in Chapter 1's voice: the first is under-edited, the second is over-theorized.

## 1. Argument

**Thesis:** A person has only so many hours, and fewer focused ones, to take ideas in; how much each hour carries depends on structure they've already built and on what they *want* to spend it on; both are trainable, which is what makes the later prescriptions possible.

**Does it earn it?** The three moves (finite → trainable capacity → want as gate) are sound and short. What the chapter does not do is *show* anything. Chapter 1 shows a thermometer; Chapter 2 shows a bacterium. Chapter 3 has one image (the tablespoon of diamonds, borrowed) and one analogy (the tutor, 63). Everything else is assertion in abstract nouns: "structure," "budget," "capacity," "want," "substrate."

**Sag points.**
- 11–17: trauma-and-lens digression. It doesn't connect to anything later and the color-filter analogy (15) is confused (a filter removes a color for the wearer; the sentence says it renders *others'* perception incomprehensible).
- 25 tells the reader what Tim Urban's purpose was and that it is not this chapter's purpose. Cut.
- 29 lists four more things the analysis "can continue to go even further to discuss," then declines to discuss them, then says "That's good enough." Then 35–37 discusses them anyway.
- 57–65: decompression on demand is a forward reference dressed as a section; 65 alone is 180 words of hand-off ("the chapter hands the receiver-budget question forward to Ch 11/12 in that frame").

**Terms the reader lacks:** "complex form"/"compressed form" (37, 53, 59, 61: Ch 4/5), "integration problem" (59: Ch 9, glossed in a subordinate clause), "training function," "institutional carriers" (41: Ch 8), "substrate custody" (65: Ch 11), "medium-shapes-want loop" (49), "preconditions" (59, 61: Ch 5), "faithful LLM" (61).

**Single most valuable structural change:** Rewrite 8–29 as one 250-word section in the register of 35 (which is the best paragraph in the file), delete 11–17 and 25–29, and cut 57–65 to the tutor paragraph (63) plus one sentence pointing to Ch 11. The chapter becomes ~1,600 words with the same three claims.

### Four paragraph rewrites

**1. Lines 9–17 (original, 260 words):**
> A human only has a set amount of time to acquire information from their environment and use it to build a world-view. / This amount of time is ultimately constrained by an individual's lifetime, but the effective amount of time will always be less than this... / Take the effects of abuse or trauma for instance... / A decent analogy to this might be that all experiences shape the lens... / All that to say that humans have only a part of their life to "make sense of the world"...

Rewrite (85 words):
> You get one lifetime to build a picture of the world, and less than that in practice. The first years go to becoming a person at all. Some of what you're handed in those years, by family and culture, speeds the work up; some of it, like trauma, bends the lens in ways that never fully straighten. However it goes, the window in which you can take the world in, think about it, and change your mind is a fraction of the years you're alive.

**2. Lines 25–29 (original, 235 words):**
> The purpose of the above mentioned article is to elucidate how precious each week of your life is... it is not the purpose of this writing. / The main point here (for this section at least) is to show that there is a small window... / This analysis can continue to go even further to discuss the available waking hours... There is certainly an evidence based, scientific argument for all of this, but it suffices to say that we all have experienced it in our lives personally. That's good enough.

Rewrite (72 words):
> Urban's point is that each week is precious. Mine is narrower: the weeks in which you can take in something new, work out what it means, and pass it on are a small share of the tablespoon. And a week is the wrong unit anyway. What matters is the focused hours inside it, and how many of those it takes before something you've read once is still there a month later.

**3. Line 39 (original, 190 words):**
> Put those three together and a property of the budget falls out that the rest of the book is going to rely on. The time budget and what it can carry, taken as one constraint, is what the book calls the *receiver budget*, the term every later chapter uses. **The receiver budget is finite, but the *capacity* of the budget... is partly a function of the structure the receiver has previously built.** The more pre-existing structure, the cheaper each new piece. This is what we mean, informally, when we say someone is "well-read"...

Rewrite (88 words):
> Call the hours and what they can carry, together, the [[receiver-budget|receiver budget]]. The hours are fixed. What each hour carries is not: it depends on what you already know. A physicist reads a physics paper in an afternoon because most of it hooks onto structure she already has. I'd need a week, and I'd need to build the structure first. That's all "well-read" means. Same hours, more per hour. The budget is finite, and its capacity is trainable.

**4. Line 65 (original, 215 words):**
> That softens the *per-claim cost* given the budget rather than fixing the budget itself. Decompression on demand is, in principle, the most leveraged intervention the book points at on the receiver side... Chapter 11 spells out the conditions under which this works (the LLM has to be faithful, which requires a *substrate custody*...), and Chapter 12 takes those conditions as inputs to the design problem. For Ch 3's purposes, the relevant point is just that... the chapter hands the receiver-budget question forward to Ch 11/12 in that frame.

Rewrite (62 words):
> That doesn't give you more hours. It makes each hour go further, which is the only kind of relief the budget allows. Everything depends on the tutor being honest, and an honest tutor depends on who owns it. So the hard limit shifts: not how much a person can absorb, but who controls the thing helping them absorb it. Chapter 11 takes that up.
