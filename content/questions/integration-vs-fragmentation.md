<!-- card -->
Climate scientists and the people who distrust them, doctors and vaccine-hesitant parents: both sides hold real information, and neither can hear the other. One tempting fix is to retreat into smaller communities whose filters you can trust. That was the book's own first prescription.

The book now rejects it, for two reasons. Small networks still squeeze ideas down internally, so the problem moves without going away. And small groups with strong identity tend to become [[echo-chambers-vs-bubbles|echo chambers]], which discredit outside information.

So the question became how complex truth moves between networks that do not share background knowledge. The working answer is integration. It runs uphill, because [[network-segmentation|large networks split by default]], and both easy versions fail. A new channel between distrustful groups can deepen the split ([[polarization-via-distrust|polarization via distrust]]), and connecting everything spreads early mistakes ([[zollman-effect|the Zollman effect]]). The book settles on a shape of connection: [[versatile-expertise|specialists who can speak both languages]], [[curation-layer|curation institutions]] that survive partial trust, and attention to what audiences want to hear.

The book marks this provisionally resolved. Problems it still names: a thin supply of such people, fields that work better isolated, and how a new institution earns trust at all.
<!-- /card -->

**Status:** provisionally resolved.

> [!note]- The working answer, in the author's words
> Integration between networks that don't share preconditions, not fragmentation into smaller ones. The prescription has three legs that only work together: cultivate versatile experts inside specialist communities, build curation-layer institutions that survive polarized trust, and reform the demand side that filters even good supply.
