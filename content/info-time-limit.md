Every idea this book is about has to land somewhere: in an actual person, who has only so many hours to take it in. The limit is so obvious it's easy to walk past. But there is a hard ceiling on how much any of us can absorb in a lifetime and a softer one on how much we can absorb in a day, and the one hopeful thing about them is that the daily ceiling can be raised. This is about where the two ceilings come from and what raising one of them takes.

## A tablespoon of weeks

You get one lifetime to build a picture of the world, and less than that in practice. The first years go to becoming a person at all. Some of what you're handed in those years, by family and culture, speeds the work up; some of it, like trauma, bends the lens in ways that never fully straighten. However it goes, the window in which you can take the world in, think about it, and change your mind is a fraction of the years you're alive.

Tim Urban's [[life-in-weeks|"Your Life in Weeks"]] draws a ninety-year life as a grid of 4,680 boxes, one per week, and then makes the grid physical:

> If you multiply the volume of a .05 carat diamond by the number of weeks in 90 years (4,680), it adds up to just under a tablespoon.

Urban's point is that each week is precious. Mine is narrower: the weeks in which you can take in something new, work out what it means, and pass it on are a small share of the tablespoon. And a week is the wrong unit anyway. What matters is the focused hours inside it, and how many of those it takes before something you read once is still there a month later.

## Focused hours

Take a typical day. Sixteen waking hours, minus logistics, conversation, food, transit, and the rest of the overhead of being a human. Most of what's left is spent doing things rather than taking things in. Inside the sliver that is absorption time, only part is focused enough to build new structure; the rest lets content go past without leaving a working copy behind. One well-known estimate puts the ceiling on focused, effortful work at around four hours a day. It comes from [[deliberate-practice|Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer's 1993 study]] of violinists, whose best students practised about four hours daily and reported that more led to exhaustion rather than progress. The number is contested, and it varies with the task, the person, and the day. Call it four or five hours, and hold it loosely.

There is a small irony here worth pausing on. That same 1993 paper is the source of Malcolm Gladwell's "10,000 hours" in [[outliers|*Outliers*]]: a careful finding about the structure of practice, compressed into a slogan about its quantity. The study's authors spent years objecting to the compression. Chapter 3's evidence has already been through the pipeline Chapter 1 describes.

Four hours is the gross number. The net depends on what is being absorbed. New material that hooks onto structure you already hold is cheap: it slots in without rebuilding anything around it. New material that doesn't hook onto anything is expensive. You either build the supporting structure first or try to hold the new piece without it, and the second usually fails. Repetition matters too; something you had no structure for and saw once is usually gone by next week. This is the difference [[expert-novice-physics|Chi, Feltovich, and Glaser]] found between physics experts and novices in 1981: shown the same problems, experts sorted them by the principle that solved them, novices by what the picture looked like. The experts weren't faster because they tried harder. They had somewhere to put each new thing.

## The receiver budget

Call the hours and what they can carry, together, the [[receiver-budget|receiver budget]]. The hours are fixed. What each hour carries is not: it depends on what you already know. A physicist reads a physics paper in an afternoon because most of it hooks onto structure she already has. I'd need a week, and I'd need to build the structure first. That is all "well-read" means. Same hours, more per hour. **The receiver budget is finite, but its capacity, how much complex form a unit of time can absorb, is partly a function of the structure the receiver has already built.**

That compounds. An hour spent building structure absorbs less in the moment and makes every later hour on related material cheaper. Investment in structure pays back as absorption. It's true for one person, which is why graduate training makes someone's later reading faster, and it's true for a culture: a group that shares vocabulary and frameworks can move ideas between its members at a lower cost per head than one that doesn't. Installing that shared structure, generation after generation, is the *training* half of the institutional work [[preservation-vs-training|Chapter 8]] takes up. The [[budget-fixed-or-trainable|open question]] is where the trainability stops, and I don't have a good answer to it.

## Want decides what the budget is spent on

The budget gets spent on something, and not at random. What it goes to is what the receiver *wants* to spend it on, and want is a standing disposition: interests, identities, projects, curiosities, anxieties. Before any content reaches your processing capacity, your own gates have already picked what gets through, and those gates run on want.

Two kinds of want need separating, because they come apart. *Reflective want* is what you'd endorse if asked: the long article you meant to read, the course you meant to finish. *Operative want* is what your attention actually does at eleven at night. Most of the budget is allocated by the second kind. The [[paradox-of-virality|paradox of virality]], which Chapter 7 returns to, is this gap measured at population scale: people say they don't want outrage to spread, and it spreads anyway, because the gate that decides is the operative one.

That gap is what a medium can work on. Spend years training your attention on fifteen-second videos and you are slowly reshaping what you crave: toward more of the same, and away from the slow material that would have built the structure to make hard things easier later. Appetite and capacity decay together, each dragging the other down. I can't point at a study that shows this cleanly over a lifetime; take it as the hypothesis the rest of the book runs on. If it's right, the [[medium-shapes-want|medium-shapes-want loop]] runs through the receiver budget at two scales at once, a person's life and a population's, and any training that expands capacity without also shaping want produces people who *could* read the hard version and don't. That is why [[want-as-prime-mover|want, not capacity]], is where the receiver-side story starts.

## Decompression on demand

Until very recently the budget has been a hard constraint on everything. Training expands capacity but not hours. Popularization cuts the cost of an idea by stripping what made it precise. Institutions do both, at a cost that scales with the number of people trained.

Picture instead handing a dense research paper to a patient tutor who first teaches you whatever you'd need in order to follow it, pitched at what you already know, and then walks you through the paper itself. You still do the learning; nobody can do that part for you. But you get much further per hour than you could alone. A faithful AI assistant used that way is [[decompression-on-demand|decompression on demand]], and it doesn't give you more hours. It makes each hour go further, which is the only kind of relief the budget allows. Everything depends on the tutor being honest, and an honest tutor depends on who owns it. So the hard limit shifts: not how much a person can absorb, but who controls the thing helping them absorb it. [[ai-as-new-node|Chapter 11]] takes that up.

## Where I'm still uncertain

- **The trainable-capacity claim has no upper bound.** Some people keep expanding capacity through their working lives; most don't. Whether the ceiling is a cognitive limit or a training limit is the [[budget-fixed-or-trainable|open question]] above.
- **Four hours is one study's number.** The argument only needs the budget to be finite and the capacity trainable, not a precise figure. A careful treatment would survey the absorption-rate literature rather than lean on Ericsson.
- **Decompression can become substitution.** The tutor story assumes the receiver reads the paper. Many will read the tutor's summary and stop. How much of the leverage survives that is an empirical question, and Chapter 11's worst case is what it looks like when it doesn't.
