In 1812 three Cambridge undergraduates, Charles Babbage, John Herschel and George Peacock, formed the Analytical Society. Babbage proposed a joking title: "The Principles of pure D-ism in opposition to the Dot-age of the University." The joke was about symbols. After the dispute over who invented calculus, British mathematicians kept Newton's dots, while the Continent built its analysis on Leibniz's dy/dx.

The two notations do different amounts of work. Leibniz's dy/dx carries the chain rule and change of variables in its symbols. Newton's dots do not. This is [[notation-as-tool-of-thought|notation as a tool of thought]]: the writing system sets which steps are easy, a [[cost-asymmetry|cost asymmetry]]. The book's software example, [[matplotlib-vs-seaborn|matplotlib vs. seaborn]], makes a similar point about tools that decide what is easy to do.

The switch took decades, a case of [[path-dependence|lock-in]]. Robert Woodhouse introduced the Leibniz notation in 1803, Peacock used it in the Cambridge Tripos examination in 1817, and it was widely adopted by about 1830.

Historians caution against crediting notation alone. Newton's preference for geometric methods and the breakdown of exchange after the dispute also isolated British mathematics. The fair reading is notation plus institutional loyalty to a [[framework|framework]].
<!-- /card -->

**How solid is this?** A good illustration but multi-causal: notation, Newton's geometric methods and the post-dispute breakdown of exchange all contributed to the British lag.
