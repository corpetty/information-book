The Black-Scholes-Merton model is a formula for pricing the financial contracts called options. From 1973 to 1976, market prices fit it poorly. Then traders started using it. From 1976 to the summer of 1987, prices converged on the model, partly through arbitrage that imposed its pricing. After the October 1987 crash a lasting departure appeared, the volatility skew. Even the skew is measured against the model's flat line, and the model's own parameter, implied volatility, became the market's language for pricing options.

Donald MacKenzie, who documented these three phases, describes financial models as engines that shape markets, where a camera would only record them. This is [[performativity|performativity]], and it is [[framework-feedback|framework feedback]] in both directions: first toward the model, then away.

In the book's terms, a market price is one of the [[three-realities|intersubjective]] things, real because enough people agree on it. A model that spreads among the people who set the price takes part in making it, which the book calls [[constitutive-transmission|constitutive transmission]]. The [[gaussian-copula|Gaussian copula]] is a related case.

MacKenzie is careful. Fitting the story does not prove it, he says, and an arbitrary formula could not have made itself true.
<!-- /card -->

**How solid is this?** Compelling for options as one well-documented case, but MacKenzie himself says consistency with performativity does not prove it, and its general scope is debated.
