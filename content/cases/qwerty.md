Look at a keyboard. The top row of letters begins Q, W, E, R, T, Y. In 1985 Paul David made this layout the standard example of [[path-dependence|path dependence]], in a paper called "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY": an early, sometimes arbitrary choice gets entrenched by network effects and sunk investment. Part of the story is typing schools, which trained people on QWERTY.

A layout is worth more the more people already type on it. The book describes a similar loop for money, where each new holder adds to the value, under [[constitutive-transmission|constitutive transmission]]. A standard that old becomes a [[default-setting|default]] and stops looking like a choice, which is what the book means when it calls a medium [[selection-as-other-engine|frozen selection]].

The popular version adds that a better layout, Dvorak, lost anyway. That part is disputed. Liebowitz and Margolis argued in 1990 that the evidence for Dvorak's superiority is "both scant and suspect" and traceable largely to Dvorak himself. Defenders reply that David never claimed a market failure that could be fixed.

So QWERTY is a fair picture of coordination lock-in, and no proof of inefficiency. The [[calculus-notation|British loyalty to Newton's dots]] is another lock-in story.
<!-- /card -->

**How solid is this?** The lock-in story is disputed even though path dependence as a concept is not; use it to illustrate coordination lock-in, not proven inefficiency.
