On 29 April 2013, weeks before the fifth edition of psychiatry's diagnostic manual (DSM-5) appeared, the director of the US National Institute of Mental Health published a statement called "Transforming Diagnosis." Thomas Insel granted the manual its strength, reliability. "The weakness is its lack of validity," he wrote. Unlike the definitions of heart disease or lymphoma, its diagnoses rest on a consensus about clusters of symptoms, with no objective laboratory measure. He announced that the institute would re-orient its research away from DSM categories, toward a scheme called the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC).

The episode shows a classification shaping which research questions get asked and funded. The categories worked as a [[selection|gate]] on what counted as a study worth paying for, which is [[theory-ladenness|theory-ladenness]] at the scale of a field. Insel's contrast between consensus and lab measure is close to the book's line between [[truth-generators|truths made by agreement and truths made by reality]]. The manual's continued use is a mild case of [[path-dependence|lock-in]], and adding a second scheme is [[framework-rotation|framework rotation]].

Insel stressed that RDoC was a research framework. It was not offered as a replacement for the manual in the clinic.

**How solid is this?** A documented statement by the NIMH director, not a measured effect; RDoC was a research framework, not a clinical replacement for the DSM.
