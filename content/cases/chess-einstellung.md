Chess experts were given a position that had a familiar solution and a better one. After finding the familiar solution, the players said they were searching for something better. Their eye movements said otherwise. Their gaze kept returning to the regions of the board tied to the solution they already had.

Bilalić, McLeod and Gobet published the study in 2008. It shows the [[einstellung-effect|Einstellung effect]], first demonstrated with Luchins's water-jar problems in 1942: a method you already know blocks the search for a better one. The chess study adds the mechanism. Attention stays anchored to the known answer while the person reports looking elsewhere.

The book describes the same trap as the [[curse-of-expertise|curse of expertise]], where deep skill screens out what does not fit the familiar frame. Its remedy is [[versatile-expertise|versatile expertise]]: depth plus the habit of treating your own frame as one option. In framework terms that is [[framework-rotation|framework rotation]]. The tool-shaped version of the problem is the [[law-of-the-instrument|law of the instrument]].

The effect is robust and it has a limit. Sheridan and Reingold (2013) found that when the familiar move was an outright blunder, every expert avoided it. Attention was bent, and nobody was blinded: [[influence-not-imprisonment|influence, not imprisonment]].
<!-- /card -->

**How solid is this?** Robust, with a known boundary condition: when the familiar move was an outright blunder, all of the experts avoided it.
