"GDPR means companies have to tell you what data they collect and let you delete it." That sentence stands in for a long European privacy statute and thousands of pages of case law. It gets the central case right and the edges wrong: when the data must be deleted, who counts as a "controller" versus a "processor", when ordinary consent is enough.

In a statute every clause has a set scope, and every "shall", "may" and "subject to" does precise work a general reader cannot see. That is the [[complex-form|full version]], nearly closed to non-lawyers. The summary is the [[compressed-form|short version]]: the same trade as [[matplotlib-vs-seaborn|a powerful software tool and its easy wrapper]]. Legal language makes exact distinctions cheap for the trained and out of reach for everyone else, a [[cost-asymmetry|built-in tilt]].

Law is unusual, the book argues, in expecting both forms to coexist, with lawyers translating case by case. That puts [[preservation-vs-training-pair|preserving the full version and training its readers]] inside one profession. It also means getting the precision applied to your own situation costs money, the law's version of what the book calls [[cost-shifting|cost-shifting]]. The book marks this wealth gate as a darker pattern than its other examples.
