Ten kids sit in a circle. The first whispers a sentence to the second, who whispers it to the third, and so on. What the last child says out loud barely resembles the original. Nobody lied. Each child passed on what they heard.

The book uses the game as its plainest picture of [[transport|transport]]: the loss in every retelling. If each retelling has a 90 percent chance of keeping an idea intact, ten retellings leave about a one-in-three chance it arrives clean, and a hundred leave almost none. That compounding is why the book says a large network puts a [[complexity-ceiling|ceiling on how complicated an idea can be]] and still cross it, and why [[compressed-form|short, simple versions]] travel.

The book also says the game cheats. Its one rule is that every player must pass the message on. Real networks have no such rule. People choose whether to pass a thing along at all, to whom, and in which version. That choice is [[selection|selection]], and the game leaves it out. So the telephone game explains what happens to an idea that keeps moving. Cases like [[hammer-vs-copernicus|two books from the same printing press]] show the other half: which ideas get to move.
