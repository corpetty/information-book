An English speaker can call a pale blue and a dark blue by the same word. A Russian speaker cannot. Russian requires a choice between goluboy, lighter blue, and siniy, darker blue. Winawer and colleagues (2007) tested whether that shows up in perception. Russian speakers were faster to tell two blues apart when the shades fell into different Russian categories than when both fell into the same one. English speakers, tested on the same colors, showed no such advantage.

A second result sets the limit. When the Russian speakers did a verbal task at the same time, the advantage disappeared. A spatial task left it in place. The effect is real, and it depends on language being active in the moment.

This is the weak form of [[linguistic-relativity|linguistic relativity]], and a clean picture of [[framework-pricing|framework pricing]]: a language, like any [[framework|framework]], makes one distinction cheaper to draw, and its speakers draw it faster. Occupy the words and the edge is gone, which is why the book files the case under [[influence-not-imprisonment|influence, not imprisonment]]. The [[piraha-numbers|Pirahã number study]] points the same way.

Neighboring claims are shakier. A 2001 finding that Mandarin speakers think about time vertically failed six replication attempts.
<!-- /card -->

**How solid is this?** Among the better-supported linguistic-relativity results, but the effect is 'online' and removable by verbal interference, and other specific findings in the field are fragile.
