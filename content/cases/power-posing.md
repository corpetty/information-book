Stand like Wonder Woman for two minutes before a job interview and you will perform better. That advice comes from a 2010 study of 42 people by Carney, Cuddy and Yap, which reported that "high-power" poses raised testosterone, lowered cortisol and improved scores on risk-taking tasks. Cuddy's TED talk on it has been viewed more than 70 million times. A book followed, then corporate training.

In 2015 a replication with 200 people found no hormone effect. In 2016 Dana Carney, the lead author, publicly disavowed the work. Psychologists now treat the study as a standard example of the replication crisis. The advice is still going around.

For the book, this is the idea that ran the whole route from lab to catchphrase and then outlived its evidence. The pose is easy, flattering and free, so it scores high on [[memetic-fitness|memetic fitness]]. The correction is technical and disappointing. Wherever editors, producers and sharers decide what to pass on (the book calls this [[selection|selection]]), the original wins. The correction reached the journals and stalled in the [[bridge-zone|bridge zone]] that had broadcast the claim. Compare [[arsenic-life|arsenic-life]], which was caught early, and the [[stanford-prison-experiment-corrective|Stanford Prison Experiment]], whose correction met the same fate.
