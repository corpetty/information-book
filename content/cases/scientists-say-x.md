A scientist spends twenty years on a result. Within a day it shows up as "scientists say X". Within a week it has hardened into "X is what the good guys believe". Two different things are happening in that headline.

The first is [[transport|loss in retelling]]. The journalist shortened the paper, the headline writer shortened the article, and what fell away was the qualifications, the method, the conditions under which the claim holds. The bare claim arrives intact. Readers apply their default rule, that a stated fact is a settled fact, and take away something the researchers never said. In the book's [[three-layer-message|three-layer picture of a message]], the content survived and the key did not.

The second is [[selection|selection]]. The headline carries one fragment, and the book argues it is usually the most surprising or actionable finding, not the most important one. It was picked for [[memetic-fitness|its pull on attention]]. The rest is still in the paper.

Both readings hold at once, as the book says they do at every stage. The step from specialist to public is its [[bridge-zone|bridge zone]], and the headline is the typical [[compressed-form|compressed form]]. [[power-posing|Power posing]] shows such a headline outliving its own correction.
