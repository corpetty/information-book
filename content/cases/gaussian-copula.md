Felix Salmon's article in Wired called David Li's Gaussian copula "the formula that killed Wall Street." MacKenzie and Spears (2014), drawing on 114 interviews, tell a more careful story about the 2008 crisis.

Investment banks had a culture of no-arbitrage modelling, and the copula served as shared infrastructure inside it. Run "backwards," it yielded numbers called implied correlations. It gave traders and whole organizations a common way to agree on values and to book profit and loss. So the formula shaped coordination as much as it shaped estimates. Its blind spot was how correlations behave in a housing-wide downturn, and because the formula was shared, the blind spot was systemic.

This is [[performativity|performativity]], the pattern documented earlier for [[black-scholes|Black-Scholes]]: a model working as an engine inside a market. Values a community settles by agreement are what the book calls [[three-realities|intersubjective]], and a model that spreads by producing that agreement is doing [[constitutive-transmission|constitutive transmission]]. One defense is [[framework-rotation|using several models]], so that a disagreement between them exposes each one's shape.

"Killed Wall Street" is overstated. The copula was one embedded component of an evaluation culture and a coordination device, and the crisis had more causes than a formula.

**How solid is this?** The 'killed Wall Street' claim is overstated; the careful account treats the model as one embedded component of an evaluation culture in a multi-causal crisis, not the sole cause.
