Two charting tools for the Python programming language can produce the same picture: a scatter of points with a trend line through it. In matplotlib, the general and powerful one, the book's example takes a screenful of code, and every color, label and line is yours to set. In seaborn, built on top of it, the chart takes one line. Seaborn even adds a shaded band around the trend line that its authors thought you would want.

The short version is short because someone else already decided. For every option seaborn hides, its authors [[default-setting|filled in a default]] on your behalf. Common charts become cheap and unusual ones move out of reach: a [[cost-asymmetry|built-in tilt in what is easy]], much as [[notation-as-tool-of-thought|a notation decides which operations are easy]]. Few people master matplotlib. Many more can use seaborn.

The book calls this software stratification, its plainest picture of a trade it finds in many places: more options buy precision and narrow the audience. matplotlib plays the [[complex-form|full version]], seaborn the [[compressed-form|short one]]. The same curve shows up in [[latin-mass-vs-vernacular|church ritual]], popular science and law, and the book traces it to [[receiver-budget|how little time and background any user has]].
