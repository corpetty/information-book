A judge in a common-law country spends decades being trained before deciding anything important. Every decision is written down with its reasons. Later judges are bound by those written decisions. Several court systems run side by side, independent of each other, and the public pays for all of it.

The book reads that arrangement as a working example of the institution it argues for. Precedent keeps the full, uncompressed version of the law alive, and the long apprenticeship keeps producing people who can read it ([[preservation-vs-training-pair|preservation and training together]]). The written reasons let people who distrust a ruling check it ([[survivable-polarization]]). Public funding keeps the courts out of the attention market ([[funding-decoupling]]). The trained judges are the part that is slowest to build and hardest to repair once corrupted ([[consumer-key-vs-surface-capture|the hard-to-recover layer]]), which is why the book calls institutions like this the [[work-of-generations|work of generations]].

The case also shows the weak point the book predicts. The redundancy only protects anything while the separate courts stay independent, and political capture of who gets appointed wears that away. It is a [[curation-layer|curation layer]] that has lasted, and one that can still be lost.
