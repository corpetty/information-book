Put a dozen people in a room to crack a hard problem and let everyone blurt out a first hunch. The room tends to stampede toward whichever answer was said first and most confidently, long before the careful evidence is in. Let them think in smaller, looser clusters before comparing notes, and they land on the right answer more often.

That is the Zollman effect, named for Kevin Zollman and central to O'Connor and Weatherall's account of how beliefs spread. Densely connected people see each other's preliminary, noisy results too quickly and converge too early on whatever took the lead. So there is a best density, one instance of the [[inverted-u|inverted-U shape]] the book keeps finding.

It matters because the book wants separated communities reconnected (see [[integration-vs-fragmentation|integration or fragmentation]]), and this result rules out "connect everything." Its partner is [[polarization-via-distrust|polarization via distrust]], which shows a new channel between distrustful groups can widen the gap. Together they push the book toward a shape of connection instead of a quantity: bridges sparse enough to avoid the stampede, dense enough for good evidence to cross. The same authors supply a related result, that [[false-consensus-rational-agents|careful reasoners can settle together on a false belief]].
