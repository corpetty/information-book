<!-- card -->
Most attempts at mass persuasion fail. Hugo Mercier's *Not Born Yesterday* collects the evidence, from advertising to political campaigns. Even Nazi propaganda, he reports, worked where it confirmed what people already felt and failed badly when it ran against public opinion.

Mercier's explanation is **open vigilance**, his revision of the older term [[epistemic-vigilance|epistemic vigilance]]. Humans are open to useful information and on guard against unreliable sources at the same time, and the two evolved together. We check whether a message fits what we already believe and whether its arguments hold up. When we err, we err toward caution: more often refusing something true than swallowing something false.

The book treats this as the best objection to its own argument and gives four answers. Vigilance guards belief, and a threatening post can still seize attention. It is one pull among several when you decide what to share. It can be [[medium-shapes-want|trained down by the media you live in]]. And Mercier's evidence covers single campaigns, while the book's worry is decades of [[engagement-equilibrium|engagement-tuned media]], a claim the book admits is untested at that scale.

Mercier's deeper challenges are the [[justification-market|justification market]] and [[reflective-vs-intuitive-belief|reflective belief]].
