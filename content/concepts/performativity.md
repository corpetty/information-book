*Concept · part of [[maps-and-territories|When the map moves the territory]]*

<!-- card -->
A formula published in 1973 said what a financial option ought to cost. At first real prices matched it poorly. Then traders began pricing with it, and for about a decade real prices matched it closely. After the 1987 crash the match broke and never fully returned ([[black-scholes]]).

The sociologist Donald MacKenzie calls this **performativity**. His image is that a financial model is "an engine, not a camera": it helps drive the market it appears to photograph. When using a model makes the world more like the model, the model looks more and more correct, for a reason that has nothing to do with it being right. The reverse also happens, and use of a model can make the world less like it. MacKenzie is careful to add that no arbitrary formula could have made itself true this way.

For the book this is the best-documented case of [[framework-feedback|framework feedback]], and a close relative of [[constitutive-transmission|constitutive transmission]], where spreading a belief helps make it so. It belongs with [[looping-effects|looping effects]] and [[goodharts-law|Goodhart's law]]. It is also why the [[map-and-territory|map-and-territory warning]] falls short on its own: this map moves the ground.
