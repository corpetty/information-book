<!-- card -->
Picture millions of people waving the same flag and chanting "freedom." Ask them what the word means and you get a dozen answers that cannot all hold together. Nobody has disagreed out loud, and the crowd is already several crowds.

The book calls this network segmentation: a network fracturing into sub-groups along the seams of its shared story. It is the output of [[myth-dilution|myth dilution]]. A story simple enough for everyone to hold has lost its reading instructions, which leaves [[interpretive-latitude|a wide range of possible readings]]. Two things sort that range into camps. People read with the habits their own community gave them, and [[selection|the filters that decide what spreads]] reward the reading that best marks us from them.

The book treats this as the default gradient for any large network, which is why reconnecting groups is uphill work (see [[integration-vs-fragmentation|integration or fragmentation]]). Segmentation is the process, and [[echo-chambers-vs-bubbles|echo chambers and bubbles]] are structures it produces. When the shared story is one that makes something real, like a constitution, the split is [[constitutive-regimes|the agreement itself coming apart]]. One open doubt: inherited habits alone may be enough to segment a network, with no identity filter needed.
