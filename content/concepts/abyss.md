*Concept · part of [[the-finite-reader|The finite reader]]*

<!-- card -->
A beginner cook learns thirty recipes and feels that cooking is a finite skill: a few hundred dishes and you are done. A chef sees an unbounded space of techniques, ingredients and cuisines, where every answer opens ten new questions. The beginner thinks the room has walls.

The book calls that space **the abyss**: everything a complex field could contain, when that is more than any person or machine could ever search. Chess has rules a child can learn and, by Claude Shannon's estimate, around 10^120 possible games, more than there are atoms in the observable universe. You have to be competent to see the abyss at all.

Experts cope by compressing. Their patterns and instincts are a lossy map of ground too large to walk, which ties the abyss to the book's [[compressed-form|compressed forms]] and to [[receiver-budget|everyone's limited attention]]. It is the [[map-and-territory|map and territory]] idea at full strength: in such a field a map is all anyone has. Depth that forgets this hardens into the [[curse-of-expertise|curse of expertise]]. Depth that remembers it becomes [[versatile-expertise|the humble, bridging kind]]. The book's open worry is whether [[ai-as-new-node|AI systems]] can be built to see the edge of their own map.
