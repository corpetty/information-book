<!-- card -->
Tell people a treatment plan will save a certain number of lives for sure, and most choose it over a gamble. Tell them the same plan means a certain number will die for sure, and most now choose the gamble. In Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1981 study the split went from 72 percent one way to 78 percent the other. The outcomes were identical ([[asian-disease-problem]]).

That is the **framing effect**: logically equivalent descriptions produce different decisions. It is among the sturdiest findings in psychology. A large rerun found it in 31 of 36 separate samples, somewhat weaker than the original and clearly there.

For the book it is the cleanest proof that a [[framework]] biases thought even when nothing is hidden or false. The frame sets the [[default-setting|reference point]], and the choice follows. It is a close cousin of the book's account of what happens when a claim travels without its reading instructions: the same words, met with a different frame, are decoded into a different belief ([[truth-compression-regimes|the three truth regimes]]). Whoever writes the headline chooses the frame, which makes framing part of the [[manipulation-surface|manipulation surface]].
