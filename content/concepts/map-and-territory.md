<!-- card -->
A subway map shows the stations in order and the lines in bright colors. It does not show distances, hills or streets, and nobody minds, because nobody tries to walk by it. The trouble comes when a simplified picture is used for something it was never drawn for, and no one remembers what was left off.

"The map is not the territory" is Alfred Korzybski's 1933 warning against confusing a representation with the thing represented. George Box gave working modelers their version: all models are wrong, and some are useful. Nassim Taleb's ludic fallacy is the same mistake in one specific form, treating messy real-world uncertainty as if it had the neat odds of a casino game. Treating an abstraction as a concrete thing has its own name, [[reification]].

Every [[framework]] is a map in this sense, and so is every [[compressed-form|summary]]. The book adds two things to the old warning. A map drawn for a [[receiver-budget|reader with limited time]] is unavoidable, because [[abyss|the full territory is too big for anyone]], so the goal is to remember what was left out. And maps get acted on, at which point [[framework-feedback|the territory starts changing to match]].
