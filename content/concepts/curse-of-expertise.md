<!-- card -->
Picture a respected specialist handed a finding from outside their field. It looks unserious to them. Parts of it are literally hard for them to see. They feel no need to look harder, and when the outsider suggests framing the problem differently, it sounds like nonsense.

The book calls this the **curse of expertise** and, drawing on the author's earlier essay on expertise, names four parts: being locked into your field's paradigm, overconfidence when reaching into other fields, filtering out what does not fit, and trouble reframing a problem. It is the field-scale cousin of the lab-measured [[einstellung-effect|Einstellung effect]], where [[chess-einstellung|chess experts' eyes keep returning to the familiar solution]], and of the [[law-of-the-instrument|law of the instrument]]: a hammer finding nails.

The book's claim is that this is what [[polarization-via-distrust|polarization through distrust]] looks like inside one head. Many people each reasonably discounting outside evidence adds up to communities that drift apart. The cure it proposes is [[versatile-expertise|versatile expertise]]: the same depth, plus the habit of treating your own frame as one option. Experts who have seen [[abyss|how vast their own field really is]] tend to have that habit.
