When the Roman world came apart, books survived because a few monasteries kept copying them: funded outside the chaos, walled off from it, working slowly for centuries. They never beat the dark age. They outlasted it.

The book makes that its model. Its diagnosis is that today's [[engagement-equilibrium|attention economy]] is a stable arrangement with no villain to defeat, so "fix the platforms" asks that arrangement to dismantle itself. Hence the reframe, **survival, not victory**: institutions that keep hard knowledge alive need to go on [[preservation-vs-training-pair|preserving it and teaching people to read it]] alongside that economy without being eroded by it. That is a lower bar than replacing it.

The two aims build different institutions. One designed to win tries to be more engaging and gets pulled into a contest it will [[out-competition-of-carriers|lose]]. One designed to survive refuses the contest and [[funding-decoupling|finds its money elsewhere]], then [[survivable-polarization|plans for partial trust]] and for the [[work-of-generations|long haul]]. The book admits this may be too pessimistic about reform: cases like the Bell System breakup show that entrenched arrangements sometimes do get changed.
