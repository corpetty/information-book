A morning show drops a surprising statistic between the weather and the traffic. It is true and properly measured. You cannot do anything with it except repeat it at dinner or get it right in a news quiz.

Neil Postman, writing about television in 1985, called the quiz and the dinner-table repeat **pseudo-context**: a use invented for information that has lost its real one. The fact arrives cut off from any decision or action, and the trivia segment papers over the gap. The book's modern examples are short-form financial news, pop-psychology clips, "explained" videos and AI summaries of summaries.

The book treats it as a typical product of the [[bridge-zone|bridge zone]], where expert findings are repackaged for a general audience. It does well on [[engagement-equilibrium|engagement metrics]], since it feels like learning, and it spares the producer the harder work of putting the context back.

It differs from [[manufactured-injection|manufactured content]] such as [[astrology|astrology]], which was never measured against anything. Pseudo-context is real information made idle. You end up misled about your own ability to act on what you know. It sits close to Postman's larger worry, the Huxley side of the [[orwell-huxley-axis|Orwell/Huxley axis]], where truth is drowned in irrelevance.
