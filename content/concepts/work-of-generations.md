Universities, scientific journals, common-law courts and open-source software communities have something in common: none was built in a product cycle. Each took generations to build, and has taken generations to drift.

The book closes its prescription on that timescale. The [[engagement-equilibrium|attention economy]] it describes built itself in two decades. Institutions able to hold against it will need at least that long, and will have to be funded, staffed and politically protected the whole way. The book calls this the work of generations: rebuilding what societies already had, plus what the new media now require.

Part of the reason is people. The [[versatile-expertise|versatile experts]] such institutions run on take longer to train than ordinary specialists, and [[institutional-decay-rates|training, once it lapses, is slow to regrow]]. The rest is design: [[funding-decoupling|money from outside the attention market]] and an aim of [[survival-not-victory|outlasting the attention economy]].

The book is frank about the cost of saying so. Telling readers the job takes decades invites the reply that there is little point starting now, and its only answer is to start building anyway. It sees one possible accelerator: [[llm-as-capability-extender|AI tools run under an institution's own control]], extending the reach of the few experts there are.
