Picture everything humanity knows about music split into two piles. One holds every score ever written, safe in a vault. The other holds the living musicians who can read them. Burn the scores and the musicians drift further from the original with each performance. Lose the musicians and the vault is just paper.

The book argues that hard-won knowledge in general works this way. When [[myth-dilution|a big idea is simplified so it can spread]], two things go missing: the [[complex-form|full original]], and people able to read it. So the repair is two jobs. Preservation keeps the full version alive somewhere (archives, journals, a court's precedents). Training keeps producing people who can decode it. The book borrows Harari's word "bureaucracy" for the repair and splits it into this pair.

Either alone fails. An archive nobody can read might as well be empty. Training with nothing behind it produces people fluent in a dressed-up simplification and sure they hold the real thing. The book pictures a pump: preservation holds the pressure, training releases it. The halves also break differently: training [[institutional-decay-rates|decays faster]] and is [[capture-asymmetry|harder to repair once corrupted]], which tells [[survival-not-victory|institutions built to last]] what to defend first.
