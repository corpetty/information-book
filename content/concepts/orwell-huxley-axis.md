<!-- card -->
Picture two ways a society could lose an inconvenient truth. In the first, a book is banned and its author jailed. In the second, it stays on sale and sits unread under an endless supply of more entertaining things.

Neil Postman, in *Amusing Ourselves to Death*, named these after two novelists. The Orwell mode is control by pain: censorship, surveillance, information withheld. The Huxley mode is control by pleasure: truth drowned in irrelevance. Postman argued the West braced for Orwell and walked into Huxley.

The book sides with Postman. The careful, well-sourced account of most topics is still available. It loses at every step of [[selection|selection]] to content tuned for [[engagement-equilibrium|engagement]], and the reader's [[receiver-budget|attention]] runs out before anyone finds it. This is harder to fight than censorship. A censor is a target you can organize against, and distraction offers no one to oppose. The book gives the same reason for finding [[self-vs-external-capture|self-capture]] more stable than attack from outside.

The book flags one complication: some states run both modes at once, censoring whatever the flood does not drown.
