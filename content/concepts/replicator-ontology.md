<!-- card -->
When you retell a joke you heard last week, you do not play back a recording. You rebuild it from what you remember, in your own words.

The word "meme" was coined by Richard Dawkins on the model of the gene: culture as discrete units that copy themselves from mind to mind. The book calls that assumption the **replicator ontology**. It is the founding premise of memetics and the one critics attack hardest. A critique of memetics that the book draws on argues that cultural transmission is reconstructive, with no clean unit being copied.

The book thinks the critique largely lands. It treats the meme-as-gene picture as a [[map-and-territory|map of culture, useful in places and wrong in others]]. Like any [[conceptual-metaphor|metaphor]], it steers what you go looking for: units, copies, mutations. So the book's account of [[selection|selection]] is built to stand without it. All it needs is something observable: people pass on some versions of an idea more than others. That sorting, which the book calls [[memetic-fitness|memetic fitness]], holds whether or not ideas come in gene-like units. Reconstruction also fits the book's account of [[transport|transport]], where every retelling is a lossy re-encoding.
