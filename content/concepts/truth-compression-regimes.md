*Concept · part of [[compression-and-truth|What shortening does to truth]]*

<!-- card -->
A lab finds that a compound causes cancer in mice at doses far above anything a person would meet. On a phone feed it has become "X causes cancer." Nothing in it is a lie, yet the reader believes X will hurt them, when the study may have implied the opposite.

The book argues that shortening a claim keeps the claim and drops [[three-layer-message|the instructions for reading it]]. The reader supplies their own, and three outcomes follow. If their reading is close to the original, truth is preserved: "the Earth goes around the Sun." If it runs against the dropped caveat, truth inverts, as with the mice. Or it stops being read as a claim and becomes a team color, which the book calls orthogonality: "climate change is real" in most rooms. The [[framing-effect|framing effect]] in psychology is related: the same facts, described differently, lead people to different choices.

Which outcome wins is set by [[selection|whoever decides what gets passed on]]. The book's alarm is that [[engagement-equilibrium|feeds tuned for engagement]] favor the false and tribal versions. This is its answer to [[truth-value-placement|where truth enters the picture]]. Across a crowd all three happen at once, a spread it calls [[interpretive-latitude|interpretive latitude]].
