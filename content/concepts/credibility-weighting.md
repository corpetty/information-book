*Concept · part of [[how-people-believe|How people actually believe things]]*

<!-- card -->
You have never measured the distance to the Sun or audited your bank. What you believe about both rests on who told you. That is how everyone handles a world too big to check first-hand: a claim from a source you trust counts for more than the same claim from a stranger.

Cailin O'Connor and James Owen Weatherall, in *The Misinformation Age*, put this habit at the center of their models of how groups form beliefs. The book calls it **credibility weighting** and treats it as rational and unavoidable. No one has the [[receiver-budget|time or attention]] to verify everything.

It is also a weak point. Whoever can manufacture or buy credibility gets false evidence carried as readily as true, which makes trust a prime [[manipulation-surface|manipulation surface]]. And it gets heavier use as checking gets harder. When publishing became free, [[cost-shifting|the work of judging quality moved onto readers]], who fall back on the cheapest shortcut available: trust by familiarity.

When two groups start out slightly apart, each sensibly discounts the other's sources and the gap widens. The book calls that [[polarization-via-distrust|polarization via distrust]]. It is also why any [[curation-layer|institution that vets on readers' behalf]] depends on trust it can lose.
