<!-- card -->
An investigative story needs a reporter, an editor and a fact-checker, and all of them are paid. A viral clip costs the platform that hosts it roughly nothing: users make the content, an algorithm picks it, ads pay. Both compete for the same hour of your evening.

The book calls the result **out-competition of institutional carriers**. Journals, universities, depth journalism and careful textbooks carry real costs for every unit of attention they earn. Platforms do not. A reader with [[receiver-budget|limited attention]], and no spare time to judge quality (see [[cost-shifting|cost-shifting]]), predictably takes the free option that was also built to be [[engagement-equilibrium|as engaging as possible]]. The book treats this as a predictable response to the market people were given, and no moral failing. Nobody censored, defunded or out-argued the institutions. They were priced out.

The end state is a lopsided collapse. Institutions keep [[preservation-vs-training-pair|holding the full, careful version of knowledge]], but little attention is left to reach it. This is why the book's proposed repair starts with [[funding-decoupling|funding that does not depend on attention markets]]. The chapter adds a caveat: outside the elite, endowed core, the institutions that hold the knowledge are collapsing too.
