<!-- card -->
Someone who distrusts a Wikipedia article's slant can still open its edit history and talk page, follow the citations, and read the argument that produced the wording. They do not have to trust Wikipedia to check the part they care about.

The book turns that into a design rule, survivable polarization: build institutions that work when trust is partly broken, instead of ones that need it whole. The reason is [[polarization-via-distrust|polarization through distrust]] (from O'Connor and Weatherall): communities reasonably discount sources they trust less, the gap widens, and that mistrust will not be repaired before the institution has to do its work.

The book lists four properties. Decisions a skeptic can audit. Reputation scores whose underlying votes or citations stay open to inspection. [[versatile-expertise|Versatile experts]] working inside the institution. And several independent routes to the same answer, as with replicated lab results or separate court jurisdictions, which also slows the premature agreement described by the [[zollman-effect|Zollman effect]]. Together they amount to [[framework-rotation|framework rotation]] at institutional scale: conclusions that people starting from different assumptions can check. It is one of three rules for a [[curation-layer|curation layer]] aiming at [[survival-not-victory|survival, not victory]].
